1

What are we comparing?

Is candidate a better than candidate b, on the same rows of this dataset?

Every comparison in the system is that one question, asked of different pairs. A candidate is one configuration (model family, hyperparameters, normalize); each is trained with a few random seeds (one run for the seed-independent linear_baseline and decision_tree). The planner sets up one of two kinds of investigation:

effect — “does X help?”one factor, a few levels of it, one reference level; everything else fixedeach level vs the reference

2

The sample is an evaluation row, not a seed

The data split is fixed per dataset, so a random seed changes only weight initialisation, dropout masks and batch order. Treating seeds as the sample (a t-test over 3 runs per condition) measures only that optimisation noise — and ignores the larger source of uncertainty: the evaluation set is a finite sample of rows.

Source of variationSize on diabetes_risk.csv (1,575 validation rows)
Spread of MLP accuracy across seeds (3 seeds, default MLP)≈ 0.6 pts
Standard error of one accuracy from the finite row sample, $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ at $p \approx 0.79$≈ 1.0 pts

A seed-based test would call a 0.3-point difference "highly significant" when it is well inside what a different draw of rows could produce. So the statistics resample rows. Seeds are still used — averaged, to smooth out initialisation noise — and their spread is reported separately as stability.

3

Per-row scores, averaged over seeds

Every training run scores every validation and test row:

$$s_{r,i} = \begin{cases} \mathbb{1}[\hat y_{r,i} = y_i] & \text{classification (1 = correct)} \\ (\hat y_{r,i} - y_i)^2 & \text{regression (squared error)} \end{cases}$$

For a condition $c$ with successful runs $R_c$, the row score is the average over its seeds:

$$\bar s_{c,i} = \frac{1}{|R_c|} \sum_{r \in R_c} s_{r,i}$$

The mean of $\bar s_{c,i}$ over rows is exactly the condition's accuracy (or MSE). A single-run condition, like linear_baseline, simply has $|R_c| = 1$ — no special case.

4

Paired bootstrap confidence interval

Both conditions are scored on the same rows, so they are compared row by row:

$$d_i = \bar s_{a,i} - \bar s_{b,i}, \qquad \hat\Delta = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i$$

$\hat\Delta$ is the accuracy (or MSE) difference. Pairing removes the variation from rows that are easy or hard for every model, which is why the interval is much tighter than comparing two independent accuracies would give.

The bootstrap

Draw $n$ rows with replacement, take the mean of their $d_i$, repeat $B = 2000$ times. The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of those means are the 95% confidence interval:

$$\text{CI}_{95\%} = \left[\, q_{0.025}\big(\hat\Delta^{*}_1, \dots, \hat\Delta^{*}_B\big),\; q_{0.975}\big(\hat\Delta^{*}_1, \dots, \hat\Delta^{*}_B\big) \,\right]$$ 
d = a_rows - b_rows
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)                    # reproducible
means = d[rng.integers(0, n, size=(B, n))].mean(axis=1)
low, high = np.percentile(means, [2.5, 97.5])
The bootstrap needs no normality assumption and works the same for 0/1 correctness and for squared errors.

No p-values are reported: the interval already says how large the difference plausibly is and whether its sign is settled, which is what a practitioner actually needs.

5b

Leader and contenders (selection mode)

The leader is the candidate with the best validation mean. A candidate is a contender unless its interval against the leader lies entirely on the worse side of zero:

$$L = \arg\max_c \; \sigma\, m_c, \qquad \mathcal{C} = \{\, c : \text{verdict}(c \text{ vs } L) \neq \text{worse} \,\}$$

($\sigma = +1$ for accuracy, $-1$ for MSE.) Only contenders may be refined, one knob at a time; when every contender belongs to one family the loop stops by itself, because tuning cannot change which family is best. These validation intervals are uncorrected on purpose: they steer the next experiment and are never reported as a claim.

6

Train, validation, test — and the sealed test set

SplitShareUsed for
train70%fitting the models
validation15%every decision inside the loop: results table, leader and contenders, what to refine, which candidate wins
test15%once, at the end: the winner vs the reference, and in selection mode vs the best candidate of another family

The loop looks at validation results after every round and adapts to them. Testing repeatedly on the data you adapt to — "run more until p < 0.05" — inflates false positives. Here that cannot happen: the confirmatory comparison is on rows the loop never saw, and the comparisons are chosen from validation before looking. With $k$ comparisons (1 in effect mode, up to 2 in selection mode) each interval is computed at $1 - 0.05/k$ — 95% or 97.5% (Bonferroni), so the claims share a 5% error budget. The report also gives the winner's test score with its own interval and how much it dropped from validation to test (selection optimism). Validation results in the UI are labelled exploratory.

7

What gets excluded (almost nothing)

A run is excluded only if it is invalid: it crashed, or produced NaN/Inf. That check lives in run_experiment. A run that is merely unusual — an unlucky seed — stays in: it is part of the condition's real behaviour, and removing it would shrink the spread and overstate certainty.

The sanity floor for classification is the majority-class rate (always predicting the most common class), shown next to every result. On a 60/25/15 dataset that floor is 60%, not the $1/k = 33\%$ "chance" level.

8

What this does not capture

Resampling evaluation rows conditions on the one training split: it says how a comparison would vary on different evaluation data, not how it would vary if the models were retrained on different data. Capturing that needs k-fold cross-validation (with a corrected resampled t-test), which multiplies training cost and is deliberately out of scope.
